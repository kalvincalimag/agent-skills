# Sound and language

Load this at step 3 when choosing between candidates that pass the principles, and whenever a candidate borrows a word from another language or the audience does not speak English. Sound is a tiebreaker. A name with a strong metaphor and slightly mismatched sounds beats a name with ideal sounds and no story.

## How sounds feel

Speech sounds carry loose associations, built from every word a listener already knows. The patterns below hold for English and its close relatives. Many do not transfer to other languages, so check them against the audience's language.

| Sound type | Feels | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stops: p, b, t, d, k, g | Solid, decisive, strong | Docker, Kafka, Git, Bolt |
| Fricatives: f, v, s, z, sh | Fast, smooth, flowing | Figma, Vite, Stripe, Zoom |
| Nasals: m, n | Warm, friendly, soft | Miro, Notion, Medium |
| L | Light, clear | Linear, Loom |
| R, and clusters such as dr, gr, br | Energy, weight, power | Rust, React, Drone |

Vowels change the felt size of a name, and this pattern holds across many languages. Most people match the made-up word "bouba" to a round shape and "kiki" to a spiky one.

- Front vowels (ee, i, e) feel small, quick, and precise: Stripe, Vite, Wiz.
- Back vowels (oo, o, aw) feel large, heavy, and serious: Vault, Zoom, Drone.
- A mix feels balanced and approachable: Notion, Figma.

Endings leave an aftertaste.

- A hard stop (t, k, p) is clean and decisive: Slack, React.
- An open vowel is friendly and open: Figma, Miro.
- A nasal lingers warmly: Notion, Zoom.

Stress placement matters too. Stress on the first syllable feels commanding (SEN-try, DOCK-er). Stress on the second feels flowing (ver-CEL). One syllable has the most impact: Vault, Slack, Rust.

## Matching sound to character

| Character | Lean toward | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Powerful, infrastructure | stops, back vowels | soft nasals, a cute -y ending |
| Fast, performance | s, v, z, front vowels | heavy clusters, back vowels |
| Friendly, consumer | m, n, l, open vowels | harsh stop clusters |
| Precise, developer | hard consonants, short syllables | soft, trailing sounds |
| Secure, trustworthy | stops with back vowels, one syllable | bouncy, open endings |
| Playful | bouncy rhythm, repeated sounds, open vowels | heavy stops at the end |

English onset clusters also carry meaning from the words that share them. gl- reads as light (glow, gleam, glint). str- reads as tension and strength (strong, stretch, strike). fl- reads as light movement (flow, fly, flash). gr- reads as grip and roughness (grip, grind, gravel). cr- reads as breaking (crack, crash, crisp). sw- reads as sweeping motion (swing, sweep, swift). sp- reads as spreading (spark, spray, spring).

## Sound check for a shortlist

1. Say each name aloud five times. Does it sit naturally in the mouth?
2. Do the main consonants match the product's character?
3. Does the vowel weight match: light and quick, or heavy and serious?
4. Does the ending stop cleanly or trail off, and which serves the name?
5. Say it in a sentence: "Check the ___ dashboard." "We switched to ___."
6. If the main audience is not English-speaking, redo these checks in their language.

## Borrowing foreign words

A foreign word can name a concept English lacks, such as kaizen, hygge, or wabi-sabi. It also brings risks. Before using one, check all of the following.

### Can the audience say it?

One unfamiliar sound is distinctive. Two is friction. Three is a wall. Modify the word or pick another.

- **Easy for English speakers.** Japanese, Italian, and Spanish mostly use familiar sounds and open syllables. Japanese long vowels and Italian double consonants get flattened, which rarely matters.
- **Workable with care.** French silent letters cause misspellings. German umlauts and the ch sound get dropped. Portuguese nasal vowels are unfamiliar.
- **Hard.** Mandarin and Cantonese tones do not survive romanization, so borrow the meaning, not the sound. Arabic throat sounds have no English match. Polish and Czech consonant clusters stop most English readers.

### Does it mean something bad anywhere that matters?

1. Confirm the meaning with an authoritative dictionary or a native speaker, not machine translation alone.
2. Scan the major languages of the target markets.
3. Check neighbor languages of the source, where shared words drift apart: Spanish, Portuguese, and Italian, or Czech, Slovak, and Polish.
4. Say it aloud in different accents and listen for an offensive sound-alike.

Siri sounds like the Japanese word for buttocks. The Chevy Nova story ("no va" means "doesn't go" in Spanish) is mostly a myth, and the car sold well. Checking is cheap either way.

### Diacritics and scripts

- Register the plain-ASCII form as the domain, handle, and package name. Use the accented form only as a display name.
- Keep a diacritic only when the market types it natively, when it is the brand's identity, or when dropping it changes the meaning.
- Avoid ß, ð, þ, ł, and ø in names meant to travel. Systems render them inconsistently.
- For non-Latin scripts, use the romanization the audience expects: Hepburn for Japanese, Pinyin for Mandarin, Revised Romanization for Korean, and readable informal spellings for Arabic and Hindi.

### Does the word do work?

A foreign word must add meaning that an English word could not. "We used kaizen, continuous improvement, because the product is a continuous-improvement tool" passes. "Japanese sounds cool" fails. Warning signs:

- You chose the language before the word.
- You cannot explain how the meaning maps to the product.
- The word means something generic, such as "light" or "flow", so you moved the thesaurus to another language.

A borrowed word is legitimate when the language has a concept English lacks, when its meaning maps to the job, when the product targets that language's speakers, or when English has already half-adopted the word (cache, emoji, avatar).
