# What makes a name work

Load this before generating candidates. Every finalist must pass these gates. They are heuristics drawn from how well-known product names behave, not laws, and step 6 weighs them against each other.

## The order of the gates

Check a candidate in this order. When it fails an earlier gate, the later ones do not matter.

1. It tells a story you can say in fifteen seconds.
2. It plants a concrete image.
3. It sounds right spoken aloud.
4. A listener can spell it after hearing it once.
5. It is short enough to stick.
6. It is distinct from the names around it.

## 1. A metaphor, not a description

The most memorable names map a concrete image onto an abstract job. Hearing "Sentry", you see a guard at a gate, and the guard explains error monitoring. Hearing "SiteWatch", you see nothing, because it only labels the category.

A metaphor borrows meaning from another domain. A description restates the category. Both can tell people what the product does, but only the metaphor gives memory something to hold.

Test: can you draw the name? Docker (a dock worker moving containers), Terraform (shaping land into something habitable), and Helm (where you steer a ship) can be drawn. CloudGuard, DataSync, and SiteWatch cannot.

## 2. Real words beat invented ones

A known word put to a new use is easier to remember than a coined one. Real words, in rough order of strength:

- **A real word.** Stripe, Vault, Linear, Slack, Notion, Craft.
- **An invented word with a root people recognize.** Figma (figure), Deno (an anagram of Node), Redis (an acronym for remote dictionary server that reads as a word).
- **An invented word with no root.** Syncora, Brightrix, Nexagen. Each is unique, and each is interchangeable with the others.

An invented word has to work harder. Keep it short, easy to say, and built from a part people already know.

## 3. Both halves of a compound earn their place

In a compound, each half must add a specific image or meaning. Cover one half and ask whether the other still says something specific. Then do the same for the other half.

- Datadog, PagerDuty, and Mailchimp pass. Every half is concrete, and the pairing is unexpected.
- CloudSync, SmartHub, and AutoFlow fail. Either half could be swapped for a dozen others without changing anything.

When a compound starts with Smart, Auto, Cloud, AI, Data, Cyber, Next, or Digital, the first half is almost always filler. Use the second half alone, or find a better first half.

## 4. Short

- One or two syllables is best. Three is the practical limit for a single word.
- Four to six letters is best for a single word. An invented word should stay at seven or fewer.
- Names over three syllables get shortened by their users. Pick a name that needs no shortening, or one whose natural short form is good.

## 5. The phone test

Say the name once to someone. They should be able to repeat it, spell it closely enough to search for it, and remember it the next day.

Spellings that drop vowels or swap letters fail this. Flickr gets typed as "Flicker" and Lyft as "Lift". A misspelling now reads as "the real spelling was taken".

## 6. A fifteen-second story

Every finalist needs an origin story you can tell in fifteen seconds. "Docker: a docker loads and unloads cargo, and Docker loads and unloads software in containers." The story aligns the team, gives marketing something to retell, and proves the name fits. "It sounds modern" is a mood, not a story. If you cannot tell the story, return to the metaphor.

## 7. Distinctive beats descriptive

A name's job is recall. The tagline, the product page, and the onboarding explain what the product does. Descriptive names feel safe and disappear into their category: TeamChat, GroupVoice, NotePad Pro. Distinctive names such as Slack, Discord, and Notion say little about the category at first, and after one use they are tied to the product for good.

The exception is a small product in a search-driven directory, such as a plugin store, where a concrete compound that hints at the job can work: Wordfence is a fence for WordPress. Even there, the memorable plugins are distinctive, and the tagline does the describing.

## Era words date a name

Words that mark the current wave of technology date the product. "e-", "i-", and "Cloud" each marked an era and now read as old. "AI" is on the same path. Remove the era word. If the name still works, it was never needed. If the name collapses without it, the name was never strong enough.
