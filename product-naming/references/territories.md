# Finding territories

Load this at step 2. Names come from territories: clusters of related images borrowed from outside the product's category. Explore territories before writing a single name.

## The technique

### 1. Reduce the product to its core job

Write one sentence: "This product [does what] for [whom]." Describe the essential job, not the features.

- Not: "It aggregates RSS feeds, filters by keyword, summarizes with AI, and posts to a chat app."
- But: "It watches the web and brings you the news that matters."

The verbs open the territories. "Watches" opens sentries, lookouts, radar, and owls. "Brings" opens couriers, heralds, and carrier pigeons. "Watches the web" opens spiders, nets, and trawlers.

### 2. Ask the six questions

Answer each one against the core job.

1. What does this job in the physical world? A lighthouse watches for ships.
2. What does it in nature? An owl hunts at night. A spider waits in its web.
3. What tool or machine does an analogous job? Radar scans. A sieve filters.
4. What role or trade does it? A herald announces. A curator selects.
5. What is the dramatic version? A canary warns miners. A tripwire sets off an alarm.
6. What is the quiet version? An undercurrent moves under the surface. A root network moves nutrients underground.

### 3. Map territories

Group the answers into territories and list a handful of concepts under each. Keep the two or three richest: the ones with the most concrete, specific images that still connect to the job.

### 4. Cross territories

The most distinctive names often take the job from one territory and the image from another. Error monitoring sits in security territory, where guard, watch, and sentinel are worn out. Borrowing from mining gives Canary, which is more vivid and more specific.

| Product | Territories crossed | Concept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Error monitoring | security and mining | Canary |
| Container orchestration | sailing and computing | Helm |
| Build pipeline | metalworking and computing | Forge |
| Log aggregation | weaving and data | Loom |
| Database | geology and storage | Bedrock, Slate |

## Borrowing from culture

Mythology, literature, science, and history carry centuries of meaning, so a reference that fits lends the name depth. A reference that does not fit is costume jewelry. It earns its place only when all four of these hold.

1. **The story maps to the job.** Prometheus brought fire, meaning light, to people in the dark, and a monitoring system brings visibility to dark systems. Ansible, in Ursula K. Le Guin's fiction, is a device for instant communication across any distance, and the configuration tool reaches any number of servers. "Zeus was powerful" is an adjective, not a mapping.
2. **The word works without the reference.** It sounds good and is easy to say for someone who has never heard the story. The reference is a bonus layer.
3. **It is specific, not the first pick.** Zeus, Apollo, Athena, Phoenix, Thor, Odin, Einstein, Tesla, and Nova are taken many times over. Go one or two levels deeper.
4. **It passes the "so what?" test** in [anti-patterns.md](anti-patterns.md).

| Domain | Worn out | Deeper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greek myth | Zeus, Apollo, Athena, Hercules, Phoenix | Prometheus, Daedalus, Mnemosyne, Argos |
| Norse myth | Thor, Odin, Loki | Huginn (the thought raven), Bifrost (the rainbow bridge) |
| Science | Einstein, Newton, Tesla | Curie, Faraday, Kepler, Lovelace |
| Fiction | Sherlock, Gandalf | Ansible |
| Space | Apollo, Galaxy, Nebula, Nova | Lagrange, Aphelion, Sidereal |

Less-used source domains: Norse and Celtic myth, Japanese concepts such as kaizen and kintsugi, Arabic and Persian science words such as algorithm and zenith, sailing (helm, keel, berth), architecture (keystone, lintel, buttress), music (cadence, timbre, octave), and geology (basalt, flint, shale). Before borrowing a word from another language, read the foreign-words section of [sound-and-language.md](sound-and-language.md).

The best references work on three levels. The word sounds good. The surface meaning says something. The story rewards whoever looks it up. Bluetooth has all three: a short, visual compound, and a tenth-century king who united warring factions, as the technology unites devices.

Before committing, search "[name] app" and "[name] software". Dozens of hits mean the reference is occupied.
